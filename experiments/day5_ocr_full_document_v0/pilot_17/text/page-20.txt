Breeding the Mali or Kulikoro Firefinch
By fan Hinze
{All Rights Reserved)

On 16 July 2000 [ received an
e-mail message which said "Clive
Barlow has found some Mali Fire-
finches Lagonosticta virata in the
UK and suggests you would like
to breed them. Clive plans to
return to Gambia next month and
has no way to keep birds in the
UK on a long term basis.

A dealer has them, Clive has
examined them; we are working
on the vocabulary and Clive has
an edited tape of their songs and
calis which he can fet you have.
We have tapes of an indigobird
that mimics the calls and songs
and would like to get begging
calls of the Firefinch chick to
compare with those of the male
Indigobird.”

The message had come from
Professor Robert Payne of the
University of Michigan, and the
‘Clive Barlow in question was
none other than the co-author of
Birds of The Gambia and Sene-
gai. At the time, | was making
sound recordings of nestling Dy-
bowski's Twinspots Euschis-
tospiza dybowskii for Prof.
Payne. undoubtedly the world's
leading authority on Indigobirds
and their parasitisation and mim-
icry of estrildid finches, and we'd
become good cyber friends.

Clive Barlow was over in Eng-
land for a month to visit his
mother in Preston, Lancashire,
and to take advantage of the nu-
merous species of birds found in
dealers’ establishments up and
down the country by recording

their songs and calls. While visit-
ing one such dealer down in
Swindon he happened upon a
cageful of African or Biue-billed
Firefinches (Lagonosticta rubri-
cata) - and was more than a little
interested to spot a number of
Mali Firefinches amongst them.
The male African Firefinch of the
race L. r. polionota is almost
identical to the Mali Firefinch, but
the two can be separated beyond
any doubt by comparing the outer
primaries of the wing. The outer-
most {second} primary in £. r
polionota is emarginated or
blade-like while that in L. virata is
broad. After notifying the proprie-
tor, Mr. Neil Eatwell. Clive was
allowed to separate the Mali Fire-
finches and these were placed in
a cage of their own. Altogether.
seven individuals were caught
up.

Clive related to Neil how rare
the species was in captivity and.
knowing of my success with a
number of waxbill species and
the work I'd been doing for Prof.
Payne, suggested that | be con-
tacted to prevent them from
“disappearing” and in order for
serious research to be carried
out. Fortunately. | have known
the Eatwell family for around 15
years and so Neil needed litte
persuasion.

arrived back home with seven of
